# Presenting the wealth-gap figure

Figure: `figures/wealth_gap_education.png`. Numbers are medians in 2025 dollars,
from the Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF), 1989–2025.

## Opening line

"Since 1989, the typical family headed by a college graduate has become much
wealthier, while the typical family headed by someone with high school or less
has barely moved. The gap between them grew from about 4 to 1 to about 6 to 1."

## Main points, with numbers

1. **One line rises, the other stays flat** (left panel).
   - College: $321k in 1989, $505k in 2025, up about 57% after inflation.
   - High school or less: $78k in 1989, $81k in 2025, up about 4%. It never
     went above $95k (in 2007).
   - In dollars, the gap went from $243k to $424k.

2. **The ratio rose by about half** (right panel).
   - 4.1× in 1989 and 6.2× in 2025.
   - The low point was 2.8× in 1995 and the peak 6.9× in 2013.

3. **Most of the widening happened between 1995 and 2013.**
   - The ratio climbed from 2.8× to 6.9× over those years.
   - Since then it has moved between 5.2× and 6.9×, and those differences are
     within the noise.

4. **The 2008 financial crisis hit the less-educated group harder.**
   - From 2007 to 2013, the college median fell 24% ($508k to $386k).
   - The high-school-or-less median fell 41% ($95k to $56k).
   - College families were back to their 2007 level by 2022. The other group
     still hasn't regained its 2007 peak.

5. **The bands support the widening, with one limit.**
   - 1995 and 2025 clearly differ: 2.4–3.3× against 5.1–7.7×.
   - 1989's range (3.0–5.6×) overlaps 2025's, because 1989 is the survey's
     smallest sample.
   - So "widened substantially since the mid-1990s" is safer than "widened
     since 1989".

## Caveats to say out loud

- **The groups changed size.** High school or less fell from 56% of families to
  33%; college grew from 23% to 41%. The people still without a degree are a
  smaller and different group than in 1989, so part of the trend is about who
  is in each group, not just how each group's wealth changed.
- **This is not the payoff from a degree.** It describes the gap; it doesn't
  show what a degree causes. Family background, inheritances and where people
  live all overlap with education.
- **Age matters.** The widening is concentrated among heads aged 55–64, where
  the ratio went from 3.3× to 11.4×. Among heads under 35 there's no widening.
  See `AGE_CHART_NOTES.md`.
- **"Head of household" is one person.** It's the survey's reference person,
  not both partners and not the head's own parents. Say "families headed by a
  college graduate".
- **These are typical families.** Medians describe the family in the middle of
  each group and ignore the very wealthy.
- **Some wealth isn't counted.** The survey's net worth leaves out Social
  Security and traditional employer pensions. Those make up a larger part of
  what less-educated families have, so the chart likely overstates the gap in
  total resources.
- **It's a survey every three years.** The lines join points three years
  apart, and nothing is measured in between. Single years are noisy, so focus
  on the trend.
- **The data are current.** The numbers are in 2025 dollars, adjusted by the
  Fed, and the 2025 survey came out on October 9, 2026.

## If you get one question

"Isn't it just that college grads are older now?" Partly: the age mix did
shift. But within the 45–54 and 55–64 age groups the gap still widened, so age
doesn't explain it all.
